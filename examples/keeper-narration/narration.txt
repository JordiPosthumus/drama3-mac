The keeper rose before dawn, as he had for thirty years, and climbed the spiral stairs with a lantern in one hand and a thermos in the other. At the top, the great lamp still turned, sweeping its slow arm across a sea that had not yet decided between grey and gold. He logged the wind, wiped the glass, and watched the fishing boats slip out of the harbour one by one, each of them a small black stitch in the morning water.

By noon the town below had woken properly. Children ran along the seawall with paper kites, the bakery sent its warm smell up the cliff path, and the keeper, now off duty, sat on his bench with bread and cheese and the crossword he never finished. Tourists asked him the same three questions about the light, and he answered them the same three ways, and everyone went home pleased.

In the evening the weather turned. Clouds stacked up over the horizon like unposted letters, and the wind found its old argument with the shutters. The keeper climbed the stairs again, checked the lamp, and stood a while at the rail, listening to the first drops hit the glass. The light would sweep all night, and the boats, he knew, were already home.
